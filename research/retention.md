## Findings by topic

### 1. Reward prediction error and reinforcement schedules
- Dopamine neurons fire for more reward than predicted, stay at baseline for fully predicted rewards and dip for less than predicted (R1). Design reading: a reward the player already fully expects (the same +25 coins every time) stops being a "surprise"; variety in *what* and *how big* matters more than frequency.
- Hopson (R2): variable-ratio schedules give "the highest overall rates of activity", fixed-ratio schedules give a pause after each reward that grows with the ratio, and "sudden loss of reward is very aversive". His recipe for long play: "make sure that there is always, always a reason for the player to be playing", plus multiple activities so a dip in one is covered by another.
- Caveat: R2 and R3 apply animal-lab schedules by analogy. R4 argues compulsion loops work by degrading the player's tools and selling the way back to baseline ("similar to drug withdrawal"), which is exactly what Gloop Pop must not do.
- Gloop Pop already has a natural variable-ratio reward in the core loop (when a combo, Mega Pop or All Clear happens depends on the deal and the player). Extra randomised payouts are not needed; extra *goals* are.

### 2. Compulsion loop vs core loop
- R3 defines a compulsion loop as "a habitual, designed chain of activities that will be repeated to gain a neurochemical reward". The fair version for Gloop Pop: core loop (place, clear, pop) feeds a goal loop (targets, missions, stars) that pays in things that make the next run more interesting, never in power needed to stay at baseline (R4).

### 3. Near-miss
- In a slot task, near-misses were "less pleasant, but increased desire to play" and recruited win-related striatal and insula circuitry; the effect on desire was "restricted to trials where the subject had personal control" (R5). This is gambling research.
- Royal Match tunes levels "towards the creation of near-misses that create pressure on players to spend" (R14). That is the rigged version. Gloop Pop's rule stays: near-miss lines only describe what really happened (design §13).

### 4. Goal gradient, endowed progress, Zeigarnik
- Goal gradient: café reward-card members bought coffee more often as they neared the free coffee; "interpurchase times decrease by 20%" (R6).
- Endowed progress: a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled was redeemed 34% of the time vs 19% for a blank 8-stamp card (same 8 purchases needed), and those customers came back 2.9 days sooner between visits (R7). The effect depends on a stated reason for the head start (R7).
- Zeigarnik: the 2025 meta-analysis finds no memory advantage for unfinished tasks (weighted ratio 0.99, N = 38 publications) but a reliable tendency to resume them (Ovsiankina effect, 67.00% resumption, N = 21) (R8). So: "unfinished goals nag you" is not supported; "people go back to finish what they started" is.
- Design reading: show progress bars that start partly filled for a real reason (e.g. today's first mission counts the run you just played), show "x of y" counts, and end sessions with a visible unfinished goal the player can resume next time.

### 5. Streaks (Duolingo, first-hand data)
- Learners who reach a 7-day streak are 2.4× more likely to come back the next day (R10) and 3.6× more likely to complete their course (R9).
- Making any lesson extend the streak (not only hitting the daily goal) raised the number of learners on a 7+ day streak "by over 40%" (R10). Lower bar, more streaks.
- Streak Freeze: letting learners hold two freezes instead of one raised daily active learners by 0.38% (R9); a new streak animation raised new-learner D7 retention by 1.7% (R9).
- Over four years, streak/leaderboard/notification work raised current-user retention (CURR) by 21% and DAU 4.5× (R11).
- Duolingo openly uses loss aversion for long streaks (R9). Gloop Pop already has a streak with freezes (§11); keep it forgiving.

### 6. Daily/weekly quests and passes
- Guild Wars 2's Wizard's Vault: new daily and weekly goals drawn from a reshuffled "deck" for variety; rewards bought from a store in any order; designed "rather than feeling like a list of chores" and so players don't "feel like they're missing out or are forever behind if they miss a day" (R25). That is the model for a free, no-FOMO mission system.
- Commercial battle passes are "primarily retention and an engagement mechanism", priced $5–$15 on mobile (R26). Gloop Pop has no paid anything, so only the free-track idea (goals → visible track of rewards) applies.

### 7. Flow and mastery
- Players have different flow zones; good games let players pick their challenge through choices built into play rather than hidden difficulty adjustment (R24).
- King: "if a level is really hard, it should also be really short"; "crazy hard levels never pay off, at least in the long term"; designers should disentangle difficulty from fun and keep pruning the least fun levels (R16). New blockers come in on a "complexity staircase" (R17).
- Mario 3D Land: stages introduce a concept, develop it, twist it, then conclude (kishōtenketsu); an assist (the White Tanooki suit) appears after repeated deaths on a stage (R27).

### 8. "One more go" in endless games
- 2048: "free, addictive and basically endless"; players "blame themselves for getting stuck in the loop"; the top request was undo (R21).
- Suika: an obvious core tension and short games; Oger credits a design choice that shortens games and reduces spamming (R22).
- Vampire Survivors: every run "banks gold toward the Power Up shop regardless of how it ends"; 27 permanent upgrades; runs capped at 30 minutes so "one more attempt never competes with a genuinely long time commitment" (R23).
- Balatro: short, self-contained runs built on familiar cards; the interview focuses on emergent synergy rather than reward schedules (R20).
- Pattern: short runs, instant restart, every run pays toward something persistent, and the failure feels like the player's own (R21–R23).

### 9. Levels, maps, stars, win streaks (block puzzle and match-3)
- Block Blast's Adventure: 96 levels over 7 days revealing a picture one pixel per level, with no reward or gallery; its win-streak counter has no rewards (R18). Reviewers call the lack of rewards a missed opportunity (R18).
- DoF: the block-puzzle winners added "levels instead of endless runs. Goals instead of pure survival", while keeping score chase; the second generation uses static, designed boards with mini-puzzles inside (R19).
- Royal Match's Butler's Gift: winning up to three levels in a row fills a bar, and "for every level won, the game places up to 3 power-ups into the board at level start" (R14). The Super Light Ball needs 10 straight wins, is lost on a failure, and raised revenue "by more than 1.5 times" (R15). R15 also warns it "greatly increases the chances of player burnout". These are built to sell extra moves; the fair version is a streak bonus that is small, never lost to anything but a fair loss, and never sold.
- Candy Crush: level 65 caused the most churn; harder levels raised conversions short term but cost players (R16).

### 10. Retention benchmarks
- 2025 global medians: D1 about 22%, D7 just under 4% (R29). Top 10% D1 about 40% (R29).
- Top-quartile puzzle games, North America, Q1 2024: D1 32.40%, D7 11.67%, D28 4.54% (R28). Puzzle "consistently maintains solid performance across all regions" (R28).
- Gloop Pop has no analytics (by design), so these are context only.

### 11. Ethics
- Dark patterns are patterns "used intentionally by a game creator to cause negative experiences for players without those players' consent"; named examples include Playing by Appointment, Grinding and Pay to Skip (R12).
- UK government position: loot-box purchases should be unavailable to children unless a parent enables them, with spending controls and transparency (R13). Gloop Pop has no purchases at all; keep it that way.
- Guardrails for this pass: no loot boxes or random paid rewards, nothing for money, no timers that punish absence (missions roll over or simply refresh; nothing is lost), no rigged near-misses or rigged deals, no fake "x% of players beaten" numbers (R18 notes Block Blast shows one; it is unverifiable), boosters only from earned coins and never needed to pass a level.

## Do not build
- Loot boxes, chests, spin wheels or any randomised reward with a price (R12, R13).
- Anything for real money, including "extra moves" (R14, R15 show how that design is tuned).
- Rigged near-misses or deals tuned to make players almost win (R5, R14).
- Timers that take things away for not logging in, or rewards that expire (R12 Playing by Appointment; R25).
- Fake social proof ("you beat 87% of players") (R18).
- Difficulty spikes like Candy Crush level 65 (R16).
